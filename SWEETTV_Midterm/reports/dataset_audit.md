### 4.4 Timestamp formats, ranges, and clock alignment

| Item | Viewing log | Schedule |
|---|---|---|
| Format | `YYYY-MM-DD HH:MM:SS` | `DD.MM.YYYY HH:MM:SS` |
| First timestamp | `2020-03-09T00:00:02` | `2020-03-08T23:00:00` (11–20) |

| Timezone/offset field | none | none |
| DST-aware | no (naive) | no (naive) |

The formats are **not directly parseable by one function** and must be parsed
explicitly. Neither file declares a timezone. Two pieces of evidence:

1. The 117 `duraton` mismatches are exactly the DST
   spring-forward hour (2020-03-29), which means viewing timestamps are naive
   local wall-clock, not UTC.
2. An empirical test matched each sampled viewing start to the nearest schedule
   programme start on the same channel, for candidate offsets from −3 h to +3 h.
   Best agreement was at **offset 0 s**
   (7.31% within 120 s),
   but the margin over the runner-up was only
   0.197 percentage points.
   This is **weak** evidence, not proof.

No offset has been applied. The schedule broadcast day starting at ~06:00 is a
programming convention, not a timezone offset.

## 6. Data-quality assessment (Stage 1 conclusions)

### 6.1 Is the dataset suitable for user–programme interactions?

Yes at the **channel/session** level, with conditions at the **programme**
level. There are 5,821,987 sessions from 4,914 users
over 139 active days, 98.05%
on channels that exist in the schedules. Programme-level attribution is feasible
only where a session maps to a schedule slot with `tv_show_id != 0`; because
88.91% of schedule rows carry ID 0, the programme
coverage of sessions cannot be assumed and must be measured after a mapping rule
is chosen. Channel-level interactions can be built now without that loss.

### 6.2 Is channel-based matching technically feasible?

Yes. 197 of 202
viewing channels match schedule `channel_id` values exactly, and
98.05% of viewing rows are on matched
channels. The remaining 5 channels
(113,579 rows) would need to be
dropped or mapped manually.

### 6.3 What timestamp problems must be resolved?

1. Two different string formats (ISO vs day-first European).
2. No declared timezone; both sides are naive local time.
3. DST: 117 sessions are one hour longer by timestamp
   difference than by `duraton`, all at the 2020-03-29 transition.
4. Schedule periods meet at broadcast-day boundaries, not midnight; date ranges
   overlap at both seams even though slots are disjoint.
5. Sessions can cross midnight, and the maximum session is 95.0 h,
   so time-only matching must handle multi-programme spans.
6. No offset may be applied until the clock question is confirmed.

### 6.4 How much of the schedule has usable programme IDs?

- 11.09% of rows (381,892 of 3,444,007).
- 16.53% of scheduled airtime.
- 9,671 distinct programme IDs across all periods.
- Per period: 89.24% unlabelled, 88.69% unlabelled, 88.82% unlabelled.
- Weeks 31–42 contain 1,437 IDs never seen in
  earlier schedule periods.

### 6.5 How much metadata is available for content-based filtering?

- Title and category: **100%** of rows.
- At least one genre: 42.67% of all rows;
  ~95% of labelled rows.
- Production year: ~10.99% of all rows;
  ~99% of labelled rows.
- Director/actors: ~9.59% of all rows;
  ~87% of labelled rows.
- Title text is episode-specific and unstable; category is the most reliable
  categorical attribute, followed by genre/year/cast on labelled rows.

### 6.6 What problems might affect a programme co-view graph?

- Only 11.09% of schedule rows have a programme ID, so
  many sessions can only be nodes at channel level.
- 17 exact duplicate rows and
  122 repeated `(user, channel, start)`
  records can inflate co-view counts.
- Very long sessions (up to 95.0 h) can span many programmes
  and create spurious co-view edges if attributed naively.
- 208 schedule channels have no
  viewing at all; 5 viewing channels
  have no schedule.
- 1,437 test-period programme IDs are new, so
  graph edges cannot be learned for them from history.
- With 4,914 users, shared household accounts and device-level
  IDs may mix preferences; the data provides no household key.

### 6.7 What cold-start cases exist?

- **New programmes:** 1,437 IDs in weeks 31–42
  never appear in weeks 11–20 or 21–30 (21.65% of test IDs).
- **Unlabelled programmes:** 3,062,115 schedule rows with ID 0 cannot
  be represented in an ID-based item matrix.
- **Channels:** 208 scheduled channels
  have zero viewing; 5 viewed channels
  have no schedule.
- **Users:** none in the template — all 1,260 template users
  appear in the log.

### 6.8 What must be investigated before constructing interactions?

1. Confirm clock semantics (local time + DST) and decide how to normalise
   timestamps without inventing an offset.
2. Choose a session→schedule mapping rule (start-time match vs interval overlap)
   and measure how many sessions map to a non-zero programme ID.
3. Decide the deduplication policy for the 122
   repeated keys and 17 exact duplicates.
4. Handle DST-crossing sessions and sessions longer than a programme.
5. Decide whether unlabelled schedule slots are dropped or kept as channel-level
   events (do **not** choose a watch-duration threshold yet).
6. Verify programme-ID stability and metadata conflicts before using item features.
7. Investigate the 5 unmatched channels, especially channel 1174.
8. Establish a leakage-safe split: weeks 21–30 have both log and schedule; weeks
   31–42 have a schedule but **no viewing log**, so no test-period interactions exist.
